      Good  Data Visualization

Reference - https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/node/320071

Government budgets, especially in the United States, are often very complicated and hard for regular people to understand. There are many numbers, programs, and technical terms, which makes it difficult to see where tax dollars go. In 2016, during President Barack Obama’s time, The White House made an interactive treemap to show the U.S. federal budget. This chart grouped government programs into colored sections, making it easier to understand how money was spent. We don’t know if this was the first interactive budget the U.S. government released, but it is still available online and helps people see the budget more clearly, even though the treemap itself is simple.

The most important part of this visualization is the way it was shared. It wasn’t the most creative treemap ever, and it wasn’t the first government budget visualization. For example, the New York Times made an interactive budget chart in 2013, and politician Ross Perot used charts to explain financial issues. What makes the White House version special is that a major world government officially used interactive data visualization to communicate with the public. People could now explore the budget themselves, instead of trying to read long, confusing documents.

This shows why making data easy to understand is important. Even a simple treemap can help people see how government money is spent. By using a clear and interactive visualization, the U.S. government made a complicated topic accessible. It helped citizens better understand government spending and set an example for transparency. This interactive budget remains a good example of how visualizations can make difficult information easier for everyone to understand.


Bad Data Visualization
 Reference : https://flowingdata.com/2016/10/21/trump-bar-chart-baselines-are-the-worst-baselines-sad/

 Data visualization is meant to show information clearly, accurately, and honestly. But sometimes, charts and graphs are designed in ways that can mislead people instead of helping them understand. One example is the Donald Trump campaign, which has been criticized for using bar charts that distort poll results. The numbers in these charts are correct, but the way the bars are shown can exaggerate differences and make Trump look better in the polls than he really was.

A common problem with these charts is that the bottom of the bar chart doesn’t start at zero. Normally, the zero point helps people see the true size of each bar. When the baseline is removed or faded, or the scale is changed, bars can look much longer than they should. This makes small differences look much bigger and can give a false impression of who is ahead in the polls.

John Muyskens, writing for the Washington Post, pointed out that the Trump campaign often used this trick. By changing the scale or cutting off the bottom of the chart, the campaign highlighted good results while hiding less favorable ones. The bars end up being more like decoration than actual information—they are designed to catch attention rather than accurately show the data.

Another problem is inconsistency. Sometimes the scale changes between charts, or the intervals on the axis are uneven. This makes it hard to compare results across different polls. A chart might show a big lead in one poll and a small lead in another, even if the numbers are almost the same. Without a proper zero baseline and consistent scale, viewers can’t correctly judge the differences.

This example shows why it’s important to design charts ethically. A good bar chart should start at zero, have even intervals, and reflect the real differences in the data. Misleading charts can make people misunderstand the numbers and form wrong opinions.

Start Bars at Zero

The bottom of a bar chart should always start at zero. This makes the length of each bar match the real value and stops small differences from looking bigger than they are.

Use the Same Scale

All charts should use the same scale on the axis. If the scale changes, it is hard to compare results from one chart to another.

Show Only Important Data

Too many bars make a chart confusing. Focus on the main points to make it easier to understand.

Label Bars Clearly

Each bar should have a label or value so viewers can see the exact numbers without guessing.

Avoid Fancy Effects

Effects like 3D or shading can make bars look longer or shorter. Keep the chart simple so it shows the data correctly.

Highlight Important Results

Use colors or notes to show the most important points instead of changing the scale to exaggerate results.

Add Context

Include a clear title, legend, or description to explain what the chart shows. This helps people understand the data correctly.
